Around the second month, the adrenaline is gone and the hard part begins. Disaster mental health experts describe common phases: a **heroic** phase right after the event, a **honeymoon** phase when people pull together, a long **disillusionment** phase when help seems slow and exhaustion sets in, and finally **recovery**. Knowing these phases are normal helps people ride them out instead of believing something is wrong with them.

Morale is survival gear. A household that keeps routine, rest and purpose makes better decisions, argues less and stays healthier.

## Priorities
1. **Keep a daily routine.** Fixed times to wake, eat, do chores, learn, rest and sleep. Routine lowers stress for adults and children. See [[K-05]].
2. **Protect sleep.** Warm, dark, quiet sleep spaces and a regular bedtime. See [[H-10]].
3. **Talk to children honestly and simply.** Tell them what is happening, what the family is doing about it, and that adults are in charge. Give them jobs and lots of play. See [[K-06]].
4. **Practice stress first aid.** Notice who is struggling, listen without fixing, connect them to support. See [[K-07]].
5. **Handle conflict early.** Hold a short family meeting each day to plan and air problems before they explode.
6. **Make meaning and fun.** Faith, music, stories, games, shared meals and small celebrations matter. Mark birthdays and holidays.

## What goes wrong
- **Disillusionment turning into despair.** Watch for people who stop eating, stop getting up, or talk about hopelessness. Take talk of suicide seriously. If phones work, call or text **988** (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline). Get the person to a doctor, clergy member or trusted neighbor, and do not leave them alone if they are at risk.
- **Alcohol and substance use rise.** Drinking to cope worsens sleep, judgment and safety around fire, fuel and tools. People who stop drinking heavily all at once can have dangerous withdrawal and need medical help.
- **Household conflict and abuse.** Close quarters and stress raise tension. Domestic violence is never acceptable. Police and shelters still respond.
- **Grief ignored.** Loss of a person, a pet, a job or a sense of safety needs space. See [[K-12]].
- **Information overload or none at all.** Limit rumor and doom talk; set a time for news once or twice a day. See [[C-09]].

## Low-power entertainment
Books, library loans taken before the outage, cards, board games, puzzles, instruments, singing, storytelling, crafts, journaling, and outdoor games in daylight. A deck of cards weighs nothing and runs forever.

## Gear tiers
| Need | Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Routine | Posted daily schedule | Schedule plus chore chart and family meeting | Shared routine with neighbors, rotating activities |
| Entertainment | Cards, paper, pencils | Board games, books for each age, puzzles | Instruments, craft supplies, story nights with the block |
| Support | Knowing who to talk to | Stress first aid basics learned | Trained volunteer, clergy or counselor connected to the block |
| Comfort | Favorite foods saved for hard days | Treats rationed for celebrations | Planned holiday and birthday supplies |

## Day-90 check
- Is everyone sleeping, eating and getting up?
- Who in the household seems different from a month ago?
- Are children playing and learning?
- What has the family done for fun this week?

## Michigan notes
Winter darkness and cold in west Michigan make low mood more common even in normal times. Get outside in daylight whenever you can, even briefly. Community Mental Health of Ottawa County runs crisis services; Michigan 211 can connect people to help.

> **Renters:** Thin walls mean conflict is public and noise travels. Agree on quiet hours with neighbors and use shared spaces for games and gatherings.

**Who's most at risk:** People with existing mental illness or substance use problems, people living alone, caregivers carrying too much, children, and anyone who has suffered a loss during the outage.
