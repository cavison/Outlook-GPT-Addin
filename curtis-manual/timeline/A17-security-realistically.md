Movies show disasters as everyone-for-themselves chaos. Decades of disaster research tell a different story: most people stay calm, help each other, and share. Neighbors are usually the first rescuers. Widespread organized violence is much rarer than fiction suggests. That said, property crime and theft can rise, especially of generators, fuel and vehicles, and stress can lead to arguments and domestic conflict.

Good security in a long outage is mostly quiet and boring: do not draw attention, lock up, know your neighbors, and talk problems down before they grow. It also means staying inside the law. Self-defense law still applies when the lights are out.

## Priorities
1. **Keep a low profile.** Use blackout curtains or only light inner rooms, keep generator noise to necessary hours, do not post on social media about your supplies, and do not stack empty food boxes at the curb. See [[X-01]].
2. **Harden the house sensibly.** Strong deadbolts, long screws in strike plates, secured sliding doors, locked garage and sheds. See [[X-02]].
3. **Know your neighbors and watch together.** A neighborhood watch with simple rotations and signals is the strongest security tool you have. See [[X-03]] and [[K-01]].
4. **De-escalate first.** Most conflict can be calmed with a calm voice, space, and a way for the other person to save face. See [[X-04]].
5. **Know the legal limits.** Learn Michigan's self-defense rules before you need them. See [[X-07]].
6. **Know when to leave.** Set triggers in advance (no heat in winter, no water, fire, flood, threats you can't manage) and keep go-bags ready. See [[X-05]] and [[X-06]].

## What goes wrong
- **Treating every stranger as a threat.** Most people at your door need help or information. Fear can lead to tragic mistakes, including shooting someone who meant no harm.
- **Showing off weapons or supplies.** It makes you a target and frightens neighbors you will need.
- **Generator and fuel theft.** Chain the generator, lock fuel, bring things in at night.
- **Unsafe firearm storage.** With children home all day and adults stressed, keep any firearms locked and unloaded when not in direct control. Michigan law requires safe storage of firearms where a minor is likely to be present.
- **Rumors.** False stories of looting gangs spread fast and can cause panic. Check before you act. See [[C-09]].
- **Waiting too long to evacuate.** Leaving in daylight with a plan is far safer than leaving at night in a crisis.

## Legal limits in Michigan (plain summary)
Michigan's Self-Defense Act generally allows deadly force only when a person honestly and reasonably believes it is necessary to prevent imminent death, great bodily harm, or sexual assault. Protecting property alone does not justify deadly force. Laws are detailed and change; this is not legal advice. Learn the law from a qualified instructor or attorney. See [[X-07]].

## Gear tiers
| Need | Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doors/windows | Deadbolts used, long screws in strike plates | Door reinforcement kits, bar for sliding doors | Reinforced frames, security film on ground-floor glass |
| Light discipline | Blankets over windows at night | Blackout curtains | Blackout plus motion lights on battery or solar |
| Watch | Know your immediate neighbors | Block watch with radios and schedule | Organized watch, sign-in for strangers, shared lookout posts |
| Evacuation | One go-bag per person | Go-bags plus written routes and destinations | Pre-arranged host family outside the area, vehicle fuel reserved |

## Day-90 check
- Has there been any incident, and was it handled calmly and legally?
- Is the watch rotation sustainable, or are people burned out?
- Are go-bags still packed and evacuation triggers still agreed?
- Is the household still on good terms with the block?

## Michigan notes
In winter, cold is a bigger threat than crime: a home without heat may be an evacuation trigger. Ottawa County Sheriff's Office and local police continue working during outages; report crimes when you can reach them, and keep a written log when you can't.

> **Renters:** You usually cannot change locks or install hardware without permission. Use removable door bars and window blocks, and coordinate with other tenants. Know your building's exits by touch in the dark.

**Who's most at risk:** People living alone, older adults targeted by scams or theft, households with firearms and children, and households that make themselves visible by noise, light or display.
