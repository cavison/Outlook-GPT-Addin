The power is back, but recovery takes weeks or months. Claims have to be filed, food and fuel replaced, damage repaired, and people need time to rest and recover emotionally. This is also the best moment to learn: you will never know more about what your household needs than you do right now.

## Priorities
1. **Document losses and file claims quickly.** Photograph damage and spoiled food before throwing it out, keep receipts, and call your insurer early. Ask whether your policy covers food spoilage or equipment damage. If the President declares a federal disaster for your county, register with FEMA at DisasterAssistance.gov or by phone. See [[V-05]].
2. **Ask the utility about credits.** Michigan utilities may offer bill credits for extended outages under Michigan Public Service Commission rules; check with your utility and the MPSC for current terms and deadlines.
3. **Hold an after-action review.** Within two weeks, sit down as a household: what worked, what failed, what we'd do differently. Write it down. See [[V-06]].
4. **Restock in priority order.** Water, medications, light and batteries, food, fuel, then everything else. Do not panic-buy; spread purchases over time. See [[F-04]] and [[F-03]].
5. **Recover emotionally.** Rest, return to normal routines, and watch for lasting stress symptoms. Get help for anyone who is not bouncing back. See [[K-07]].
6. **Keep the community going.** Thank neighbors, return borrowed gear, and keep the block contact list and meeting habit alive.

## What goes wrong
- **Throwing out evidence.** Spoiled food and damaged items tossed before being photographed can't be claimed.
- **Contractor fraud.** Unlicensed contractors ask for big deposits and disappear. Check licenses with the State of Michigan (LARA), get written estimates, and don't pay in full up front.
- **FEMA impostors.** FEMA does not charge to apply. Real inspectors carry ID and will not ask for bank passwords.
- **Forgetting the lessons.** Within a few months, people go back to empty pantries and dead batteries. Put restocking on a calendar.
- **Hidden stress.** Nightmares, irritability, trouble sleeping and drinking more can show up weeks later. These are common and treatable.

## Gear tiers
| Need | Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Records | Photos of damage and receipts | Written loss list with dates and costs | Home inventory (photos/video) kept off-site before the next outage |
| Review | Quick family talk | Written after-action notes | Updated paper plan and gear list from the review |
| Restock | Replace what you used | Replace and add what you lacked | Rotation calendar and budget for steady restocking |
| Recovery | Rest and routine | Stress first aid check-ins | Connection to counseling or faith community for anyone struggling |

## After-action questions
- What ran out first? What did we have too much of?
- What was the scariest moment, and what would have prevented it?
- Which gear did we actually use every day? Which never left the box?
- Which neighbors helped, and how do we stay connected?
- What skill do we wish we had learned beforehand?

## Day-90 check (after recovery)
- Claims filed and tracked.
- After-action review written and the paper plan updated. See [[A04]].
- Supplies restocked at least to where they started, ideally better.
- Everyone in the household sleeping and functioning normally, or getting help.

## Michigan notes
The Michigan Public Service Commission oversees outage credits for regulated utilities; rules and amounts change, so check current terms. Michigan State Police Emergency Management and Homeland Security Division coordinates state disaster assistance. Ottawa County Emergency Management and Michigan 211 can point you to local recovery help.

> **Renters:** Your landlord's insurance generally covers the building, not your belongings. Renters insurance may cover spoiled food or damaged property; check your policy. Document problems with the unit in writing and keep copies.

**Who's most at risk:** People without insurance, older adults targeted by contractor and FEMA scams, households that return to no supplies, and people quietly struggling with stress after the crisis.
