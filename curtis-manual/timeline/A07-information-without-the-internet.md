In a long outage, good information is as important as food. You need to know how big the outage is, where help is, and what is true. Cell towers have backup batteries and some have generators, but many go quiet within hours to days. Plan to get news the old way.

**What's happening now:** phones still show a few bars, maybe. Radios work. Rumors are already starting.

### AM/FM and NOAA Weather Radio

Broadcast radio is the backbone. Big AM stations can be heard over long distances, especially at night, and many stations have backup generators. A **NOAA Weather Radio** receives National Weather Service alerts — critical in ice storms, blizzards and heat waves. Get a radio that runs on regular batteries, with a hand crank or solar panel as a backup ([[C-01]]).

Write down your local stations in the binder ([[A04]], [[Z-04]]).

### Keep phones alive

- Turn on airplane mode or low-power mode; turn off Bluetooth, location and background apps.
- Lower screen brightness.
- **Text instead of calling.** Texts use less power and network capacity and often get through when calls fail.
- Charge from power banks, a car (running outdoors, never in a garage), or solar panels ([[C-02]], [[P-08]]).
- Download offline maps and save key documents before you need them.

### Two-way radios

- **FRS** ("walkie-talkies") — no license, short range, good for family and the street.
- **GMRS** — more power and range; needs an FCC license (no test; covers your family).
- **CB** — no license, used by truckers; range varies.
- **Ham (amateur) radio** — longest range and organized emergency nets; **you need an FCC Technician license to transmit** (FCC Part 97). Get licensed before you need it ([[C-07]]). Cheap Baofeng-type radios are popular for ham use but are not FCC-certified for FRS/GMRS.

Agree on family channels and check-in times ([[C-08]]).

### Rumor control

Rumors spread fast in a crisis: "the water is poisoned," "the National Guard is taking generators," "power is back on in Grand Rapids." Before you act:

1. Who said it? Did they see it, or hear it?
2. Is it confirmed by an official source (county, state, utility, NWS)?
3. Do two independent sources agree?
4. What happens if you wait an hour to check?

Do not repeat what you have not checked ([[C-09]]).

### Where official information appears

When the internet is down, officials fall back on radio, printed notices, and in-person points: fire stations, township and city halls, schools, churches, libraries, and community centers. In Ottawa County, emergency management works with townships and cities; expect bulletins at those places. In Ukraine during the blackouts, public warming and charging points became information hubs too. Pick the nearest one and check it daily.

## Priorities
1. Battery or crank radio on, tuned to a local station and NOAA.
2. Phones in power-save mode; text, don't call.
3. Family radio channel and check-in times set.
4. Identify your nearest official information point.
5. Check before you act on rumors.

## What goes wrong
- Phones drained on day one by scrolling.
- No way to hear storm warnings during an ice storm or blizzard.
- Families act on rumors and drive across the county for nothing.
- People transmit on bands they are not licensed for.

> **Who's most at risk:** people who are hard of hearing, people who live alone, non-English speakers, and anyone who only gets news through a smartphone. Check that they hear the news too.

> **Renters:** No yard or wood stove? A radio near a window works best in apartments. Post a building bulletin board in the lobby and share news with your floor.

## Gear tiers
| Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|
| Battery AM/FM radio, power bank | NOAA weather radio with crank/solar, FRS radios, car charger | Licensed GMRS or ham radio with good antenna, solar charging setup |

## Day-90 check
Do you have a steady news routine that does not depend on the internet — radio at set times, a daily trip to the information point, and a street message board?
