By the second week the flashlight scramble is over and a new problem shows up: everything you own wants to be charged, and you cannot charge all of it. In a west Michigan winter, darkness falls before 5:30 p.m. in December, so you may face 14 or more hours of dark every day. In summer the days are long, which helps solar panels, but heat makes people want fans you cannot run.

This chapter is about deciding what deserves light and power, and producing it without burning the house down or poisoning anyone.

## Priorities
1. **Safe light first.** Put an LED headlamp or lantern within reach of every person, every night. Battery LED light should be your default; flame is the backup. See [[Q-08]] and [[P-01]].
2. **Protect power for life-safety uses.** Medical devices (oxygen concentrators, CPAP, nebulizers, powered wheelchairs), insulin cooling, and one way to receive news and call for help come before anything else. See [[K-09]] and [[M-09]].
3. **Make a daily power budget.** Write down every device, its watts, and how many hours you will run it. Total the watt-hours and compare to what you can produce or store. See [[P-09]].
4. **Run the generator on a schedule, outdoors, 20+ feet from the house.** Cycling a freezer a few hours twice a day keeps it cold for far less fuel than running nonstop. Never run a generator in a garage, even with the door open. See [[P-04]] and [[P-05]].
5. **Never backfeed.** Plugging a generator into a wall outlet with a "suicide cord" can electrocute line workers and neighbors and start fires. Use a transfer switch or interlock installed by a licensed electrician, or plain extension cords to individual appliances. See [[P-06]].
6. **Rotate and protect batteries.** Store spares cool and dry, use oldest first, and keep a charging routine. See [[P-03]].
7. **Use the car carefully.** A running car can charge phones and a small inverter, but only outdoors, never in an attached garage, and with the tailpipe clear of snow. See [[P-08]].

## What goes wrong
- **Candle fires.** Candles left burning near curtains, in kids' rooms, or while people sleep are a leading cause of house fires during outages. If you use flame, one adult watches it and it is out before bed.
- **Carbon monoxide.** Generators, car engines in garages and fuel-burning lanterns indoors kill people every major outage, often in the first few days but also later when people get tired and careless. Battery CO alarms on every sleeping level.
- **Generator theft.** A loud generator advertises itself. Chain it to something solid, run it during daylight when you can, and bring the cords in at night if you can do so safely. See [[X-01]].
- **Running out of fuel by week three.** Gas stations need power to pump. Stored gasoline goes stale in months without stabilizer. Plan for shortage, not abundance. See [[R-08]].
- **Dead batteries in a drawer.** Alkalines leak, rechargeables self-drain. Check them now.
- **Overloading extension cords.** Use heavy-gauge outdoor cords sized for the load; a warm cord is a warning.

## Gear tiers
| Need | Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | LED headlamp per person, plus spare batteries | Rechargeable LED lanterns, one per room in use | Lanterns plus solar-charged battery bank |
| Charging | Car charger, 2–3 phone power banks | Portable power station (about 500–1,000 Wh) | Power station plus 200+ W folding solar panels |
| Backup power | None; rely on car and batteries | Portable inverter generator, 30-day fuel plan | Generator with professionally installed transfer switch or interlock |
| Safety | Battery CO alarm per sleeping level | CO alarms plus ABC extinguisher | Extinguisher in each zone, written generator log |

Sizes are examples. Check each device's label for watts and use [[P-07]] to size your own.

## Day-90 check
By the end of the third month you should be able to answer yes to these:
- Every person has working light they can find in the dark.
- You know your daily watt-hour use and how many days of fuel or battery you have left.
- The generator, if any, has had its oil changed on the maker's schedule (many need oil every 50–100 hours of running) and has never run indoors.
- Nobody has backfed the house.
- Batteries are being rotated, and the dead ones are stored for proper recycling later.

## Michigan notes
Winter solar output in Ottawa County is low; short, cloudy lake-effect days can cut panel output to a small fraction of a summer day. Do not plan winter life around solar alone. Snow on panels must be brushed off. Consumers Energy and other Michigan utilities warn customers every storm season never to backfeed.

> **Renters:** You cannot install a transfer switch without your landlord's permission. Use a portable power station and extension cords run directly to appliances, ask before running a generator on the property, and never run one on a balcony or in a stairwell.

**Who's most at risk:** People who rely on powered medical equipment, households with small children near open flame, and anyone running a generator while exhausted or alone.
