Most outages in west Michigan are short. A summer thunderstorm or an ice storm takes down lines, crews from Consumers Energy work through the night, and the lights come back within hours or a few days. This book is about the rare case when that does not happen, and the power stays off for weeks or months. You do not need to be afraid of it. You need to understand it well enough to plan.

**What's happening now:** nothing. That is the point. Everything in this chapter is easier to think about with the lights on.

### How the grid works, in plain terms

The grid has three parts:

1. **Generation** — power plants (gas, coal, nuclear, wind, solar, hydro) make electricity.
2. **Transmission** — high-voltage lines on tall steel towers carry it long distances to substations.
3. **Distribution** — local substations step the voltage down, and the poles and wires on your street carry it to your house.

Supply and demand must match every second. When something breaks and the system cannot balance, protective equipment trips lines off to avoid damage, and that can spread outward. Most everyday outages are **distribution** failures: a tree on a line, a broken pole, a blown transformer on your street. These are quick to fix. Long outages come from damage to transmission, generation, or the big substation equipment that is hard to replace.

### What causes outages

- **Ice storms** — the biggest regional threat here. A coat of ice can add hundreds of pounds to a span of wire and bring down trees, lines and poles over whole counties. In late March 2025 a severe ice storm in northern lower Michigan left large numbers of customers without power, some for well over a week, and damaged forests and poles across several counties.
- **Lake-effect snow and wind** — heavy wet snow, blizzards and windstorms take down lines and make repair slow.
- **Summer thunderstorms and heat waves** — straight-line winds topple trees; heat raises demand and strains equipment.
- **Solar storms (geomagnetic disturbances)** — strong storms on the sun can induce currents that damage large transformers. In 1989 one collapsed the Quebec grid for about nine hours.
- **Cyberattack** — attacks on control systems have caused real outages (Ukraine, 2015 and 2016).
- **Physical attack** — people have shot at or sabotaged substations in the US.
- **Fuel shortages** — power plants need fuel delivered; frozen gas supply was part of the Texas 2021 failure.

### Why big transformers matter

Large power transformers at major substations are heavy, often custom-built, and mostly made overseas. Replacing one can take many months to years. Utilities keep some spares, but a disaster that damages many at once could keep a region dark far longer than a storm normally would. This is the main reason "90 days" is a plausible planning number, not a movie plot.

### The cascade

When power fails for long, other systems fail with it:

- **Water and sewer** — city pumps and treatment plants have backup generators, but only for so long. Pressure drops; boil advisories follow.
- **Fuel** — gas station pumps need electricity.
- **Phones and internet** — cell towers have batteries and some generators; many go quiet within hours to days.
- **Money** — card readers and ATMs stop. Cash becomes king.
- **Pharmacies and stores** — no refrigeration, no inventory systems, no deliveries.
- **Heat** — almost every Michigan furnace, including gas furnaces, needs electricity for the blower and controls.

### Three scales of outage

| Length | What it feels like | What matters most |
|---|---|---|
| ~3 days | Inconvenience | Light, cold food, warmth, staying informed |
| 2–3 weeks | Disruption | Water, sanitation, heat, cooking, cash, medicine |
| 90+ days | A different way of life | Community, routine, producing and preserving food, health, morale |

A 3-day kit is not a 90-day plan cut short. Long outages change what kills people: carbon monoxide, cold, heat, dirty water and missed medical care do far more harm than violence.

### Case studies

- **Northeast blackout, August 2003** — a software alarm failure and overgrown trees touching lines in Ohio started a cascade that cut power to roughly 50 million people in eight US states (including Michigan) and Ontario. Most power was back in one to two days, but water systems in Detroit lost pressure and issued boil advisories. Lesson: even a short outage can knock out city water.
- **Texas, February 2021 (Winter Storm Uri)** — extreme cold froze gas supply and power plants; operators cut power to millions to prevent total collapse, often for days. Hundreds of people died, many from hypothermia and carbon monoxide poisoning from generators, cars and grills used indoors. Lesson: cold and CO are the real killers.
- **Puerto Rico, 2017 (Hurricane Maria)** — the island's grid was destroyed. Many homes waited months for power, some close to a year. Researchers later estimated thousands of excess deaths, largely from interrupted medical care and the strain of living without power. Neighborhoods that organized — shared kitchens, generators, water points — coped better. Lesson: a long outage is a health and community emergency.
- **Ukraine, 2022 onward** — repeated attacks on power plants and substations brought rolling blackouts through winters. Cities set up heated, powered public "points" for charging and warmth, and households adapted with batteries, generators and routines. Lesson: people can live through long, repeated outages with planning and cooperation.

## Priorities
1. Understand that most outages are short, and plan for the rare long one anyway.
2. Know your utility (most of Ottawa County is Consumers Energy) and how it reports outages.
3. Plan for the cascade: water, heat, cash, phones, medicine — not just lights.
4. Start with the household audit in [[A02]].

## What goes wrong
- People plan for 3 days and are caught flat at week two.
- Families assume a gas furnace or city water will keep working. They may not.
- People expect violence and forget the quiet killers: CO, cold, and contaminated water ([[A06]]).

> **Who's most at risk:** older adults living alone, infants, people on oxygen, CPAP, dialysis or insulin, people with disabilities, and anyone in an all-electric home in January.

> **Renters:** No yard, no wood stove, no generator allowed? You can still store water and food, keep battery lights and a radio, plan one warm room, and know your neighbors. Ask your landlord now what the building does when power fails.

## Gear tiers
| Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|
| Flashlights, battery AM/FM radio, 3 days water and food | 2 weeks of water, food, heat plan, power bank | 90-day plan: stored food, water treatment, safe backup heat and power, community ties |
