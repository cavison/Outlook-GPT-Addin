In real long outages, most deaths are not dramatic. They come from carbon monoxide, cold, heat, fire, spoiled food and falls in the dark. In Texas in 2021, many of the deaths were from hypothermia and carbon monoxide poisoning. This chapter is the most important one in the book for days 1–3.

**What's happening now:** people are cold or hot, tired, and improvising heat, light and cooking. That is exactly when mistakes happen.

### Carbon monoxide

CO is an invisible, odorless gas from burning anything: gasoline, propane, charcoal, wood, kerosene.

- **Never** run a generator, grill, camp stove, charcoal or a car indoors, in a garage (even with the door open), in a basement, or on a porch near windows.
- Generators go **outdoors, at least 20 feet from the house** (CDC), with exhaust pointed away from doors, windows and vents.
- Never use a gas oven or range to heat the house.
- Put **battery-powered CO alarms on every floor** and near sleeping areas. Test them.
- **Symptoms:** headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, confusion, sleepiness. If several people or pets feel sick at once, suspect CO. **Get everyone outside into fresh air and call 911** ([[Q-02]]).

### Fire

Candles, lanterns, space heaters and stored fuel cause house fires in every big outage.

- Use LED lights. If you must use a candle, put it in a sturdy holder, away from anything that burns, and never leave it alone or near kids or pets.
- Keep heaters 3 feet from anything that can burn. Refuel kerosene heaters outside, cold.
- Store fuel outside the house.
- Have extinguishers and working smoke alarms. Plan two ways out of every room, and practice in the dark ([[H-14]]).

### Cold

Michigan winter is the biggest threat for a long outage.

- **Hypothermia signs:** shivering, clumsy hands, slurred speech, confusion, drowsiness. Older adults and infants may not shiver much ([[H-11]]).
- Pick one warm room and seal it ([[Q-05]], [[H-01]]).
- Dress in layers, cover heads, stay dry ([[H-09]]).
- Keep pipes from freezing, or drain the system ([[H-07]], [[H-08]]).

### Heat

A summer outage during a heat wave can be just as deadly.

- **Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache, cool clammy skin. Move to shade, cool down, sip water.
- **Heat stroke:** hot skin, confusion, fainting, very high body temperature. **This is an emergency — call 911 and cool the person fast** ([[H-12]]).
- Most at risk: older adults, infants and young children, people with heart or lung disease, and people on certain medicines (some for blood pressure, mental health and allergies affect heat tolerance — ask a pharmacist).

### Food poisoning

When refrigeration fails, bacteria grow fast. Throw out perishable food that has been above **40°F for 2 hours or more** (USDA). **When in doubt, throw it out** ([[F-02]]).

### Injuries in the dark

- Falls on stairs: keep a headlamp on, clear walkways.
- Chainsaws: storm cleanup injuries are common. Use chaps, eye and ear protection, and never cut alone or above shoulder height. Leave trees on power lines to the utility.
- Ladders on ice: wait for daylight and a spotter.
- Storm debris: nails, broken glass, hidden wires.

## Priorities
1. CO alarms working; no fuel burning indoors except appliances rated for it.
2. Fire safety: LED over flame, extinguisher handy.
3. Warm room set up before night (winter) or cooling plan (summer).
4. Sort the fridge with the 40°F/2-hour rule.
5. Light every path; no risky chores in the dark.

## What goes wrong
- Generator in the garage "with the door open."
- Charcoal grill brought inside for warmth.
- Candle left burning in a bedroom.
- Older neighbor with mild confusion — actually hypothermia or CO.
- Chainsaw kickback during cleanup.

> **Who's most at risk:** infants, older adults, people with heart or lung disease, people taking medicines that affect heat or alertness, and anyone sleeping in a room with a fuel-burning heater.

> **Renters:** No yard or wood stove? Never run a generator on a balcony or in a stairwell — CO drifts into other units. Put a battery CO alarm in your unit even if the building has hardwired ones. Know two ways out of the building in the dark.

## Gear tiers
| Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|
| Battery CO and smoke alarms, flashlights, extinguisher | Alarms on every floor, indoor-rated heater with ventilation, thermometer for fridge | Wood stove installed to code, chainsaw PPE, first-aid training |

## Day-90 check
Do CO and smoke alarms still have fresh batteries? Are people getting careless with flame and fuel? Fatigue makes people cut corners in month two — recheck weekly.
