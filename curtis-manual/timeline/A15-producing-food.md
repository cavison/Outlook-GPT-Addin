By the second month, stored food is shrinking and fresh food is gone. It is tempting to think a garden or the river will feed the family. Be realistic: a first-year garden adds vitamins, variety and some calories, but it will not replace stored food in 90 days. Most vegetables take 50 to 100 days or more to mature, and in west Michigan the frost-free season usually runs from about mid-May to early October. Fishing, small livestock and careful preservation help, but stored food is still your foundation.

What you can grow or catch depends heavily on when the outage starts. A January outage means sprouts, microgreens and ice fishing. A May outage means a real garden is possible.

## Priorities
1. **Start fast, small food now.** Sprouts are ready in days; microgreens in one to three weeks, any season, on a windowsill. See [[F-12]] and [[G-02]].
2. **Plant quick crops if the season allows.** Radishes, lettuce, spinach, bush beans and greens come first; potatoes and winter squash give more calories later. See [[G-01]], [[G-03]], [[G-04]] and [[G-05]].
3. **Fish and hunt only within the law.** A Michigan fishing license is required for anyone 17 and older. Follow DNR seasons and limits, and MDHHS "Eat Safe Fish" guides. See [[G-10]] and [[G-12]].
4. **Forage only what you know for certain.** The rule: never eat any plant you cannot identify with complete confidence. Never eat wild mushrooms unless an expert has identified them in person. See [[G-07]] and [[G-08]].
5. **Consider small livestock carefully.** Chickens give eggs, rabbits breed fast, but both need feed, water and time. Check Hudsonville or township ordinances before keeping them. See [[G-16]] and [[G-17]].
6. **Preserve the surplus.** Without a freezer, use drying, salting and smoking, fermenting, cold storage, and canning that follows the USDA guide exactly. See [[G-18]] through [[G-23]].

## What goes wrong
- **Expecting too much.** Families plant big gardens and stop rationing stored food. Keep eating to plan until you actually harvest.
- **Poisoning from foraging.** Several deadly plants in Michigan look like edible ones (for example poison hemlock and water hemlock resemble wild carrot and parsnip).
- **Unsafe canning.** Low-acid foods (vegetables, meat, fish, beans) need pressure canning. Botulism is rare but deadly. No guessing, no old family recipes that skip steps.
- **Thin ice.** Ice fishing on lakes and the Grand River backwaters kills people every winter. See [[G-13]].
- **Contaminated fish.** Some west Michigan waters have consumption advisories for PCBs, mercury or PFAS. Check the Eat Safe Fish guide for the specific lake or river.
- **Seeds that don't come true.** Hybrid seed saved from this year's crop may not grow the same plant. See [[G-06]].

## Gear tiers
| Need | Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growing | Sprouting jar and seeds | Containers, soil, seed packets for fast crops | Raised beds, stored heirloom seed, season extenders |
| Fishing | Hand line, hooks, license | Rod and reel, tackle, current Eat Safe Fish guide | Ice fishing gear, cleaning kit, preservation plan |
| Livestock | None | Small flock of laying hens (if allowed) | Hens plus meat rabbits, stored feed for 90 days |
| Preserving | Salt, drying screens | Water-bath canner, USDA guide | Pressure canner, dehydrator plan, root cellar or clamp |

## Day-90 check
- What percentage of meals now include something you grew, caught or sprouted?
- Is stored food still on schedule, or did garden optimism cause overeating?
- Are seeds saved (open-pollinated only) for the next planting?
- Is everything canned labeled, dated and made by a tested USDA method?

## Michigan notes
MSU Extension publishes planting calendars and soil-testing services for Ottawa County. Lake Michigan tributaries such as the Grand River have seasonal runs of fish; DNR rules apply. Trapping and hunting have strict seasons and license rules; an outage does not suspend them.

> **Renters:** Grow in containers or buckets on a balcony or patio. Ask before digging in shared yards. Most rentals ban livestock.

**Who's most at risk:** Families who stop rationing early, foragers who guess, anyone on ice alone, and anyone eating home-canned low-acid food that was not pressure canned.
