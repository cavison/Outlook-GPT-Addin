After a month without power, many normal jobs pause and new work appears. Generators need repair. Pipes split. Bikes get flats. Kids need teaching while schools are closed. Older neighbors need care. A person who can fix, sew, teach or nurse becomes valuable to the whole street. Skills do not run out, do not get stolen, and can be traded again and again.

This chapter is about building and using those skills, and trading fairly.

## Priorities
1. **Know what your household can already do.** List each person's skills: mechanical, medical, childcare, cooking, sewing, teaching, carpentry. Share it at the street meeting. See [[K-02]].
2. **Get the hand tools that replace power tools.** Hand saw, brace and bits, hammer, wrenches, pliers, screwdrivers, a pipe wrench and a good knife. See [[R-01]].
3. **Learn the high-demand repairs.** Small engines, leaking pipes, bicycle flats, and basic clothing repair. See [[R-02]], [[R-05]], [[R-03]] and [[R-04]].
4. **Keep children learning.** A short daily lesson routine keeps kids calm and on track. Teachers in the neighborhood can rotate. See [[R-11]].
5. **Offer care work.** Childcare, elder care and basic nursing free up other adults for heavy work. See [[K-08]].
6. **Barter fairly and safely.** Trade in daylight, in public or with people you know, and never show your whole stock. See [[R-06]].

## What goes wrong
- **Doing work beyond your skill.** Gas lines, electrical panels and chainsaw tree work kill amateurs. Do not trade dangerous work you are not trained for.
- **Unfair trades that breed resentment.** Charging desperate neighbors high prices damages trust you will need later.
- **Trading away what you need.** Do not barter your last medicine, fuel or water filter.
- **Advertising your supplies.** Bartering from a garage full of goods invites theft. Keep trades small. See [[X-01]].
- **Injuries from hand tools.** Tired people with axes and saws get hurt. Take breaks; work in daylight.

## What holds value
Items that commonly hold value in long emergencies: fuel, batteries, medicine and first-aid supplies, salt, sugar, coffee and tea, cooking oil, matches and lighters, soap, toilet paper, seeds, and skills. Exact values depend on what is scarce locally; there is no fixed exchange rate.

## Gear tiers
| Need | Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tools | Basic hand tool set | Hand-tool kit from [[R-01]] plus pipe and bike tools | Full shop of hand tools, sharpening gear, spare parts |
| Sewing | Needle, thread, buttons, safety pins | Sewing kit with patches and heavy thread | Treadle or hand-crank sewing machine |
| Teaching | Paper, pencils, library books | Grade-level workbooks for each child | Printed curriculum plan shared with neighbors |
| Trade goods | A few small extra items | Planned barter stock separate from family supply | Barter stock plus a skill others need |

## Day-90 check
- Has each adult learned at least one new practical skill?
- Is the children's learning routine still running?
- Are tools cleaned, oiled and accounted for?
- Have trades with neighbors been fair enough that people still trust you?

## Michigan notes
Ottawa County has many people with farm, trade and manufacturing backgrounds. Ask; the best small-engine mechanic on your street may be retired. MSU Extension and local libraries offer skill classes in normal times. Take them before you need them.

> **Renters:** Ask your landlord before doing plumbing or other repairs on the building, and document anything you fix. Your skills still trade even if you can't modify your unit.

**Who's most at risk:** People who take on dangerous repairs without training, and isolated people who have no one to trade with.
