In a west Michigan winter, a house without power cools down day by day. Without a heat source, indoor temperatures can approach outdoor temperatures within days, and pipes freeze. In summer, an upstairs room in a heat wave can be dangerous. Shelter-in-place means making a small part of your home livable and keeping your body warm or cool.

**What's happening now:** the furnace is off (even gas furnaces need electricity), or the AC is dead in a heat wave. Shrink your living space and manage it.

### One living zone

Pick one room — small, interior or south-facing, few windows, near the bathroom — and live there ([[H-01]]).

- Close off other rooms; hang blankets over doorways.
- Cover windows with blankets, bubble wrap or plastic at night; open south windows to sun by day ([[H-02]]).
- Set up a tent indoors for sleeping; body heat warms a small space ([[H-03]]).
- Put rugs on cold floors.

### Safe indoor heat

- **Wood stove or fireplace** — must be installed to code with a clean chimney. Fireplaces lose much of their heat up the chimney; inserts are better ([[H-05]]).
- **Indoor-rated propane or kerosene heaters** — only models labeled for indoor use, with a window cracked for fresh air, a CO alarm in the room, and never while sleeping unless the maker allows it ([[H-04]]).
- **Never** use a grill, camp stove, gas oven or outdoor heater indoors.

### Firewood

A wood-heated Michigan home can burn several cords in a winter; a single warm room uses far less. Wood should dry six months to a year. Split, stack off the ground, cover the top ([[H-06]]). Freshly cut storm wood will not burn well this winter.

### Body heat

- Layers: wicking base, insulating middle (wool, fleece), wind-blocking outer. Hats, socks, mittens ([[H-09]]).
- Sleeping bags rated for cold, wool blankets, hot water bottles (not too hot), and sharing a bed or tent ([[H-10]]).
- Eat and drink warm food and fluids; your body needs fuel to make heat.

### Pipes

Let faucets drip if water is flowing, open cabinet doors, insulate exposed pipes. If you cannot keep the house above freezing, **drain the plumbing** before pipes burst: shut the main, open all faucets from top to bottom, drain the water heater (power or gas off first), and pour RV antifreeze into traps ([[H-07]], [[H-08]]).

### Summer cooling

- Shade windows on the sun side; open windows at night to cross-ventilate.
- Sleep on the lowest floor; basements stay cooler.
- Wet cloths, cool foot baths, misting, and plenty of water.
- Watch for heat exhaustion and heat stroke ([[H-12]]).
- Go to a cooling center if one opens.

### Michigan notes

Ice storms and lake-effect snow often hit together with cold snaps, so a winter outage is the hardest case here. Sump pumps stop in spring thaw. Keep snow cleared from furnace and dryer vents and from generator exhausts.

> **Scenario: a 90-day outage that starts in January.** Expect February–March cold snaps and lake-effect snow with no grid. Drain the plumbing early rather than late ([[H-08]]); size firewood or fuel for 90 days in one room, not the whole house; count calories higher for cold. After an ice storm, avoid walking or parking under loaded trees and lines for days — limbs keep falling and downed lines may be live. Watch roof snow load on garages, porches and low-slope roofs; rake from the ground, never climb icy roofs, and get out if you hear cracking or see sagging. Spring thaw brings flooding basements with no sump pump.

## Priorities
1. Choose and seal one room before the first night.
2. Use only safe heat with a CO alarm.
3. Dress and sleep warm.
4. Protect or drain pipes.
5. In summer, cool the body and watch heat illness.

## What goes wrong
- Heating the whole house and running out of fuel.
- Unvented heaters, grills or ovens used for heat — CO deaths.
- Pipes burst when the house drops below freezing.
- Older adults get hypothermia slowly without noticing.
- Green firewood that smokes and won't burn.

> **Who's most at risk:** older adults, infants, people with heart or lung disease, people with dementia, and anyone living alone. In summer, add people on medicines that affect heat tolerance.

> **Renters:** No yard or wood stove? Pick the unit's warmest interior room, use a tent and sleeping bags, and cover windows. Only use heaters your lease and building allow, never fuel heaters on balconies. Units surrounded by other heated units stay warmer; ask about a shared warm room.

## Gear tiers
| Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|
| Wool blankets, cold-rated sleeping bags, window plastic, layers for all | Indoor-rated heater with fuel, indoor tent, battery fan, CO alarm in warm room | Wood stove installed to code, 2+ seasons of dry firewood, plumbing drain plan practiced |

## Day-90 check
Can you keep one room above about 50°F (10°C) through a Michigan cold snap for weeks? Is fuel or wood enough for the rest of the outage? If not, plan to share a warm space or relocate ([[Q-10]]).
