No household gets through 90 days alone as well as a street does together. One home has a well, another a generator, another a nurse, another a big pantry and no way to cook. Pooling skills and gear saves fuel, food and lives. Studies of long outages, including Puerto Rico after Hurricane Maria in 2017, found that neighborhood mutual aid groups often kept people fed, informed and cared for when official help was slow to arrive.

Community does not form by accident. Someone has to knock on the first door. It can be you.

## Priorities
1. **Hold a street meeting in the first week.** Short, outdoors, in daylight. Introductions, who needs help, what people have, and when to meet again. See [[K-01]].
2. **Map the block.** Who lives where, who is vulnerable, who has skills, generators, wells, chainsaws, radios. Keep the map private and on paper. See [[K-02]].
3. **Share big resources.** One community kitchen saves fuel compared with ten small stoves. One charging station, one water point, rotating childcare. See [[K-03]].
4. **Check on vulnerable neighbors daily.** A simple wellness routine catches problems early. See [[K-04]] and [[Q-07]].
5. **Make fair decisions.** Rotate leadership tasks, write down agreements, and give everyone a voice so a few loud people do not take over.
6. **Connect to bigger groups.** Churches, schools, township offices, the county emergency manager, and volunteer groups (Red Cross, CERT, local food pantries) can provide supplies and information.

## What goes wrong
- **A few people take over.** Without clear rules, one strong personality can make decisions for everyone. Use simple votes and rotate roles.
- **Free riders and burnout.** The same few people do all the work. Post a task schedule.
- **Sharing too much information.** A block map that shows who has food and guns should not be posted publicly.
- **Food safety failures.** A community kitchen must follow handwashing, safe water and food safety rules or it can make many people sick at once. See [[F-02]] and [[S-04]].
- **Feuds.** Old neighbor disputes resurface under stress. Keep meetings focused on tasks.

## Gear tiers
| Need | Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communication | Door-to-door visits, paper notes | Message board at a central spot, FRS radios | GMRS or licensed ham operator links to county nets |
| Shared cooking | Rotating potluck | Community kitchen with large pots and fuel plan | Kitchen plus food safety lead and sign-up schedule |
| Shared power | Phones charged at one house | Charging station with power station and solar | Generator with fuel pooled and a written schedule |
| Organization | Names and addresses list | Block map, skills list, task chart | Written agreements, rotating coordinators, link to church or township |

## Day-90 check
- Is every household on the block accounted for and checked on regularly?
- Are tasks shared fairly, or are a few people exhausted?
- Is the block connected to township, county or church relief efforts?
- What should the street keep doing after the power returns?

## Michigan notes
Ottawa County Emergency Management coordinates the county response; local churches and schools in Hudsonville and Jenison often serve as warming or distribution sites in storms. Michigan 211 lists local services. A licensed ham operator on the block can relay messages through local emergency nets. See [[C-05]] and [[C-08]].

> **Renters:** Apartment buildings are communities too. Hold the meeting in the lobby or parking lot, map the building by floor, and include the landlord or manager if they are reachable.

**Who's most at risk:** Older adults living alone, people who do not speak English, new residents who know no one, and people who are too proud or too isolated to ask for help.
