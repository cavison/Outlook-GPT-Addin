In the first week, the fridge and freezer are emptied; then you live on the pantry. Food is not usually the first thing to kill in an outage, but poor planning leads to waste, sickness and low morale. The aim is steady calories, safe cooking, and meals people will eat.

**What's happening now:** the fridge is warm, the freezer is thawing, and you are cooking on something new. Make a plan.

### Eating order

1. **Fridge first** — it goes bad soonest. Discard perishables held above 40°F for 2+ hours (USDA).
2. **Freezer next** — a full freezer holds about 48 hours closed, half full about 24. Food with ice crystals or at 40°F or below can be refrozen or cooked.
3. **Pantry last** — canned and dry goods.

In winter, a cooler outdoors in shade can hold food cold, but freeze-thaw and animals are risks ([[F-13]]). See [[F-01]] and [[F-02]].

### Rationing and calorie planning

Adults generally need roughly 1,600–3,000 calories a day depending on size and activity; hard physical work and cold raise needs. Children, teens, pregnant and nursing women have special needs; older adults need protein and fluids even when appetite drops. Use [[F-03]] and [[Z-03]] to plan. Don't cut calories hard early; hungry, cold people make mistakes.

### Cooking without power

All fuel-burning cooking goes **outdoors** unless the appliance is rated for indoor use. Options:

- **Camp stove** and **grill** — outdoors only, away from openings ([[F-08]]).
- **Rocket stove** — burns sticks efficiently ([[F-05]]).
- **Wood fire** — outdoors or in a wood stove ([[F-09]]).
- **Solar oven** — works on clear days, even in cold ([[F-07]]).
- **Haybox (retained-heat) cooker** — bring a pot to a boil, then insulate it to finish cooking with no fuel ([[F-06]]).
- A gas range burner can usually be lit with a match during an outage; never use it for heat.

### Cooking from scratch

Learn now: skillet flatbread ([[F-10]]), beans and rice from dry ([[F-11]]), oatmeal, soups and stews. Soak beans overnight to save fuel. Sprouted seeds add fresh food ([[F-12]]).

### Keeping food cold

Winter: an outdoor box, snow bank or unheated garage. Summer: ice chests (ice may be scarce), a pot-in-pot evaporative cooler ([[F-14]]), and a cool basement.

### Pests

Store dry food in sealed metal or thick plastic containers. Check often for moths and mice ([[F-15]]).

### Food fatigue and morale

Weeks of the same meal wear people down and they eat less. Spices, sauces, a treat, rotating menus, and cooking together help ([[F-16]]).

## Priorities
1. Eat in order: fridge, freezer, pantry.
2. Throw out unsafe food; when in doubt, throw it out.
3. Set up safe outdoor cooking.
4. Plan meals and calories for every person.
5. Protect stored food from pests.
6. Keep meals varied.

## What goes wrong
- Grilling in the garage.
- Eating food that warmed up too long.
- Eating pantry food first and letting the freezer spoil.
- Running out of cooking fuel because everything is boiled for an hour.
- Kids refusing food they don't know.

> **Who's most at risk:** infants (formula needs safe water — [[K-10]]), people with diabetes, older adults with poor appetite, and people with food allergies or special diets.

> **Renters:** No yard or wood stove? Check whether grills are allowed and where. A camp stove used outdoors, a haybox cooker and no-cook foods (peanut butter, canned fish, crackers) stretch your fuel. Balconies count as indoors for CO if near doors and windows.

## Gear tiers
| Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|
| 2 weeks of no-cook and easy-cook food, coolers, manual can opener | Camp stove with fuel, haybox, rocket stove, 30-day pantry | Solar oven, 90-day pantry, wood stove cooking, food preservation gear |

## Day-90 check
Does your food last 90 days with fuel to cook it? Can you add fresh food — sprouts, fish, a garden ([[A15]])? Is morale holding?
