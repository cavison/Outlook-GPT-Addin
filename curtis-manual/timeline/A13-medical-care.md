In a long outage, the big medical danger is not dramatic injury. It is small problems that grow: a cut that gets infected, diarrhea that dehydrates a child, a prescription that runs out, a sprain made worse by hauling water on it. Hospitals usually keep running on backup power, but they may be crowded, roads may be bad, and pharmacies may be closed or cash-only. Your job is to prevent what you can, treat the small stuff well, and know when to go for help.

This chapter is not a replacement for training. Take a first-aid and CPR class before you need it (the American Red Cross and American Heart Association offer them in the Grand Rapids area).

## Priorities
1. **Keep up chronic medications.** Count every prescription today. Know how many days you have. Talk to your doctor and pharmacist about a legal reserve and emergency refills. Michigan pharmacy rules may allow a limited emergency supply of some medications when a prescriber cannot be reached; ask your pharmacist what applies (check Michigan Board of Pharmacy). See [[M-10]].
2. **Prevent infection.** Handwashing, clean water for wounds, and early attention to redness and swelling matter more than any gear. See [[M-03]] and [[M-12]].
3. **Stop bleeding and keep people breathing.** Direct pressure, packing, a tourniquet for limb bleeding that will not stop, and CPR. See [[M-02]], [[M-07]] and [[M-14]].
4. **Treat dehydration early.** Diarrhea and vomiting from bad water or spoiled food are common. Oral rehydration solution saves lives. See [[M-08]].
5. **Store medicines correctly.** Insulin and some other drugs need cool storage; know the maker's room-temperature limits. See [[M-09]].
6. **Know where help still works.** Find out which hospitals, urgent cares and pharmacies have generators. Listen to local radio and county announcements.

## What goes wrong
- **Closing a dirty wound.** Sealing a wound that has not been cleaned well traps bacteria. When in doubt, clean it, cover it and leave it open. See [[M-04]].
- **Waiting too long.** People delay going in because travel is hard, then show up with a serious infection or severe dehydration.
- **Running out of a medication suddenly.** Stopping some drugs abruptly (for example certain blood pressure, seizure, steroid or psychiatric medications) can be dangerous. Ask your doctor now what to do if supply is short; do not ration on your own guess.
- **Injuries from new chores.** Chainsaws, axes, carrying water, falls on ice, and burns from camp stoves rise in long outages. See [[M-05]] and [[M-06]].
- **Tooth pain ignored.** A dental infection can become serious. See [[M-11]].

## Gear tiers
| Need | Minimum | Better | Serious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kit | Store-bought first-aid kit plus extra gauze, tape, bandages | 90-day kit built from [[M-01]] | 90-day kit plus tourniquet, hemostatic gauze, splints, trained user |
| Medications | Written list of all meds and doses | 30-day reserve arranged with doctor | 90-day reserve, rotated, with cool-storage plan |
| Knowledge | This manual | First-aid/CPR class | Wilderness first aid or Stop the Bleed course, *Where There Is No Doctor* and *Where There Is No Dentist* on paper |
| Rehydration | Salt and sugar on hand | Commercial ORS packets | ORS plus a way to make safe water in quantity |

## Day-90 check
- Every chronic medication has a plan that reaches past day 90, or a known place to refill it.
- Every wound in the household has healed or been seen.
- You still have gauze, tape, gloves and antiseptic; restock list started.
- Someone in the house can do CPR and stop bleeding.

## Michigan notes
In winter, frostbite and falls on ice add to the injury list; in summer, heat illness and tick bites (Lyme disease is present in parts of Michigan). Ottawa County Department of Public Health posts emergency health information; call 911 for emergencies if phones work. Michigan 211 can help locate services.

> **Renters:** Your medical plan does not depend on the building, but your landlord may not be able to keep heat or water running. Keep your medications and kit in a go-bag you can grab if you have to move to a shelter or family.

**Who's most at risk:** People with diabetes, heart or lung disease, kidney disease on dialysis, people on psychiatric medications, the very young and very old, and anyone injured while doing unfamiliar hard work.
