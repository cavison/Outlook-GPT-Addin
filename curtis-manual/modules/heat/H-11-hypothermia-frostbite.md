Recognize and treat body cooling and frozen skin before they become deadly.

## Signs
**Hypothermia (adults):** shivering, exhaustion or feeling very tired, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech, drowsiness.
**Hypothermia (infants):** bright red, cold skin; very low energy.
**Danger:** body temperature below 95°F (35°C) is a medical emergency. A person who stops shivering while still cold may be getting worse.
**Frostnip/early frostbite:** redness, pain, tingling.
**Frostbite:** white or grayish-yellow skin, skin that feels firm or waxy, numbness. Often fingers, toes, nose, ears, cheeks, chin.

## You need
- Dry clothes, blankets, sleeping bag
- Warm room, warm sweet drinks
- Thermometer
- Warm (not hot) water and a basin

## Steps
1. Get the person into a warm place and out of wet clothes.
2. Warm the center of the body first: chest, neck, head and groin, with blankets, dry layers or skin-to-skin contact under blankets.
3. If they are awake and can swallow, give warm, sweet, non-alcoholic drinks.
4. After their temperature rises, keep them dry and wrapped, including the head and neck.
5. For frostbite: get to warmth, do not walk on frostbitten feet if you can avoid it.
6. Put frostbitten areas in warm (not hot) water, or warm them with body heat (armpits).
7. If they are unresponsive, check breathing and start CPR if needed ([[M-07]]). Get medical help.

## Caution
Do not rub frostbitten skin or massage it with snow. Do not use a heating pad, stove, fireplace or heater to warm numb skin; the person cannot feel it burning. Do not thaw frostbite if it may freeze again before you reach shelter.

## Field note
Hypothermia does not need sub-zero weather. CDC notes it can happen above 40°F if someone is wet from rain, sweat or water. Older adults in a cold house over several days are at real risk, so check them daily ([[Q-07]]).
