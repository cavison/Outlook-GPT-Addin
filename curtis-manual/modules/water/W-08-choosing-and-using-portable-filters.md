Pick and use a store-bought water filter that will actually protect your family, and keep it working for 90 days.

## You need
- A filter labeled with an absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller, or certified to NSF/ANSI Standard 53 or 58 for cyst reduction (or NSF P231 for microbiological purifiers)
- Two buckets marked DIRTY and CLEAN
- Backflush syringe (comes with many filters)
- Bleach or a way to boil

## Steps
1. Choose: filters with pores of about 0.1–0.2 micron (common hollow-fiber squeeze and gravity filters) remove bacteria and protozoa like Giardia and Cryptosporidium. A product sold as a "purifier" is tested for viruses too.
2. Pre-filter cloudy water first ([[W-06]]) and follow the maker's instructions for flow and cleaning (backflushing).
3. Most filters do not remove viruses. For water that may hold sewage (floodwater, ditches, after a sewage spill), also disinfect with bleach or boil.
4. Keep the filter from freezing. A frozen hollow-fiber filter can crack inside with no visible sign, and it may let germs through. Store it indoors and carry it in an inside pocket in winter.
5. Keep dirty and clean sides separate. Never let dirty water drip onto the clean outlet or bottle.

## Caution
Pitcher and fridge filters (like many carbon pitcher filters) improve taste and some chemicals but are not made to treat untreated outdoor water. Don't rely on them for river, pond, or rain water.

## Field note
Many hollow-fiber makers say a filter that may have frozen should be replaced, because you can't tell by looking. In a Michigan winter outage, keep the filter in the warm room at night — and buy a spare; they're inexpensive and lightweight.
