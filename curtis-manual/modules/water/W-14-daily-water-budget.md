Figure out how much water your household needs each day and stretch it without risking health.

## You need
- Pencil and paper (or the table below)
- A marked bucket or jug for measuring
- Stored water inventory

## Steps
1. Start with FEMA's minimum: 1 gallon per person per day — about half for drinking, half for cooking and hygiene. Store at least a 3-day supply; 2 weeks if you can.
2. Add more for hot weather, nursing mothers, sick people, and children. Plan extra for pets.
3. Never ration drinking water. Drink what you need today and find more tomorrow. Cut back on washing first.
4. Reuse: dishwater and bath water go to toilet flushing. Use a sponge bath ([[S-05]]) and three-tub dishwashing ([[S-07]]) to save water.
5. Check your supply each morning and write it down. When you're down to about 3 days, make getting and treating more water the day's top job.

## Table
Minimum drinking-and-hygiene water at FEMA's 1 gallon per person per day. Toilet flushing and laundry are extra.

| People | 3 days | 2 weeks | 30 days | 90 days |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 gal | 14 gal | 30 gal | 90 gal |
| 2 | 6 gal | 28 gal | 60 gal | 180 gal |
| 4 | 12 gal | 56 gal | 120 gal | 360 gal |
| 6 | 18 gal | 84 gal | 180 gal | 540 gal |

A bucket flush uses about 1–2 gallons poured quickly into the bowl. For 90 days, plan to collect and treat water ([[W-06]]–[[W-12]]), not store it all.

## Caution
Dehydration comes on fast in heat and in sick kids. Watch for dark urine, little urine, dizziness, or dry mouth. Vomiting or diarrhea means a person needs more water, not less ([[M-13]]).

## Field note
Cold weather fools people into drinking less — but dry indoor winter air and heavy layers still cost water. Put a full cup in each person's sight at the warm-room table; people drink more when it's in front of them. A 5-gallon bucket marked inside at each gallon makes daily budgeting easy.
