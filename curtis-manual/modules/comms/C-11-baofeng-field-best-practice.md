Habits that make a handheld Baofeng last for weeks on limited power and reach as far as it can, without breaking the law.

## You need
- Baofeng radio (UV-5R class), programmed per [[C-03]]
- Spare charged battery pack, and/or an AA battery case ("battery eliminator") made for your model
- Your pocket frequency card ([[C-04]])
- Pencil and notebook for a radio log
- Optional: better antenna ([[C-06]])

## Steps
1. Set power to **Low** by default. Use High only when Low truly does not get through; high power drains the battery much faster.
2. Turn on the **keypad lock** before the radio goes in a pocket or bag, so buttons don't change your channel or key the mic.
3. Keep a **spare**: a second charged pack or an AA battery case. Rotate packs and recharge from solar or the car ([[P-07]], [[P-08]]) at set times.
4. **Listen far more than you talk.** Most of the time the radio should be receiving only; scan at scheduled times ([[C-12]]) instead of all day.
5. Keep transmissions **short**: think first, press, wait a moment, say it, release. Transmitting uses far more power than listening.
6. Don't transmit while the radio sits in its charging cradle; take it out first.
7. Hold the antenna **straight up and vertical**, not sideways, and away from your body and metal.
8. **Go high and go outside**: an upstairs window, a hill, or an open yard beats a basement or the inside of a car.

## Michigan notes
Michigan's police, fire and EMS use MPSCS (the Michigan Public Safety Communications System), a digital, trunked network. A Baofeng is an analog FM radio and **cannot hear** that traffic, so don't buy one hoping to monitor police. Use it for NOAA weather, local ham repeaters and nets ([[C-12]]).

## Caution
You need an FCC Amateur license to transmit on ham frequencies ([[C-07]]). The only exception is a real emergency with immediate threat to life or property when normal communication is unavailable (FCC §97.403). These radios are not FCC-certified for FRS/GMRS, so don't use them on family-radio channels.

## Field note
Cold weather makes lithium-ion packs look nearly empty. Carry the spare inside your coat and swap a cold pack for a warm one; the cold one often recovers after it warms up. In a cold Michigan house, charge packs in the warm room, not the garage.
