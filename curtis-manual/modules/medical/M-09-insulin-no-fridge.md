Keep insulin and other temperature-sensitive medicines usable when there is no refrigeration.

> This is not a substitute for training or professional care. Take an American Red Cross first aid/CPR class and a Stop the Bleed class before you need them. If 911 or any medical service is reachable, use it.

## You need
- Your medicine's package insert/label
- Thermometer; insulated cooler; frozen jugs (insulin must not touch ice)
- Two unglazed clay pots (one fits inside the other), sand, water — or a commercial evaporative insulin wallet

## Steps
1. Read your product's label now. Unopened and in-use room-temperature limits differ by product (many are about 28 days below about 86°F / 30°C, but some are shorter or longer). Write each limit on a card.
2. Keep insulin in use cool, out of direct sun, away from stoves and car dashboards.
3. For longer storage, build an evaporative cooler: sand between two clay pots, keep the sand wet, cover with a damp cloth, set in shade with airflow. It works best in dry air.
4. Never freeze insulin. Throw it away if it has frozen, or if it looks cloudy, clumped, or discolored when it should be clear. Write the date each vial/pen is first used or taken out of the fridge.

## Caution
Insulin that froze or overheated may look normal but not work, leading to dangerously high blood sugar. Keep extra testing supplies and know the signs of high and low blood sugar. If blood sugar is not controlled, seek emergency help.

## Field note
In a Michigan winter you can use an unheated room or a cooler in the garage — but check a thermometer daily, because a frozen vial is ruined. In a cooler with ice, wrap insulin in a towel inside a sealed bag so it never touches the ice.
