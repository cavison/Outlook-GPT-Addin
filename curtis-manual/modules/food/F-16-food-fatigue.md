Keep people eating enough during a long outage when the same plain foods start to make them lose appetite.

## You need
- Spices, bouillon, hot sauce, garlic, onion, soy sauce, sugar, vinegar
- Comfort foods: hard candy, cocoa, coffee, tea, cookies, popcorn
- Sprouts and anything fresh you can grow or barter
- A simple weekly menu sheet

## Steps
1. Plan a weekly menu that rotates at least 5–7 different main dishes.
2. Change one thing each meal: a new spice, sauce, texture or shape (flatbread, pancakes, dumplings).
3. Schedule treats: a hot drink each day and a "special" meal once a week.
4. Add fresh food when you can: sprouts, windowsill greens, garden produce, legal fish or game.
5. Let kids pick or help cook one meal a week.
6. Watch for people who skip meals or lose weight, especially elders, kids and sick people; offer smaller, more frequent foods they like.

## Caution
Older adults, young children and sick people can quietly stop eating and become weak or dehydrated. Track who is eating, and do not force rationing on them. Calories matter more than perfect nutrition in a crisis.

## Field note
Appetite fatigue is a known problem in long emergencies: people may refuse plain stored food even when hungry. A small stock of familiar flavors (your family's favorite spice blend, ketchup packets, chocolate) is worth more per ounce than another bag of rice. Eating together at set times also helps morale.
