Salt and hot smoke extend the life of fish and meat for days to weeks — but they do not make food shelf-stable like canning.

## You need
- Non-iodized salt (canning/pickling or kosher), brown sugar optional
- Food-grade bucket or glass/ceramic container
- Smoker: grill with a lid, barrel, or a box over a fire pit
- Hardwood (apple, cherry, maple, oak, hickory) — never pine or treated lumber
- Food thermometer, racks, cold storage

## Steps
1. **Start fresh and cold:** use fish or meat cleaned within hours and kept cold ([[G-11]]).
2. **Brine:** soak pieces in a salt brine (use a tested extension recipe for the strength and time) in a cold place.
3. **Rinse and dry:** rinse, pat dry and let air-dry until the surface is tacky (a "pellicle") — 1–3 hours in a cool, breezy spot.
4. **Smoke hot:** smoke with a low hardwood fire; bring the thickest part up to a safe internal temperature (follow the tested recipe — fish usually to at least 145°F/63°C, many recipes call for higher).
5. **Check:** fish is done when it flakes and is opaque all the way through.
6. **Cool and store cold:** refrigerate, keep in snow/ice, or freeze. Eat within a few days unless frozen.

## Caution
Cold smoking (below cooking temperature) and vacuum-packing smoked fish can let botulism grow. Hot-smoked fish is still a perishable food — keep it at 40°F or below. For long storage, can smoked fish only with the USDA tested method ([[G-19]]).

## Field note
Salt pulls water out of meat, which is why a heavily salted, thoroughly dried fish (like old-style salt cod) can keep for long periods. In winter, Michigan's cold air (below 40°F) makes brining and pellicle-drying much safer and easier than in summer — plan big smoking jobs for late fall.
