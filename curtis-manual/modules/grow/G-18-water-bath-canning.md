Preserve high-acid foods — fruit, jams, pickles, and acidified tomatoes — in jars using boiling water, following USDA-tested recipes only.

## You need
- USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or a tested NCHFP / MSU Extension recipe
- Large deep pot with a rack and lid (water must cover jars by 1–2 inches)
- Mason jars with new flat lids and bands; jar lifter, funnel
- Heat source you can control (outdoor propane burner or camp stove if the power is out)
- Bottled lemon juice or citric acid (for tomatoes)

## Steps
1. **Pick a tested recipe** and follow it exactly — ingredients, jar size, headspace and time.
2. **Prepare jars:** wash in hot soapy water and keep hot; check rims for nicks.
3. **Fill:** pack food, leave the headspace the recipe says, remove air bubbles, wipe the rims clean.
4. **Lid:** place flat lid, screw band on fingertip-tight.
5. **Process:** lower jars onto the rack in hot water; water 1–2 inches over lids. Start timing only when a full rolling boil returns. Keep it boiling the whole time.
6. **Cool:** turn off heat, wait 5 minutes, lift jars straight up, and set on a towel undisturbed 12–24 hours.
7. **Check seals:** lids should be pulled down and not flex. Refrigerate and eat any that didn't seal.
8. **Label and store** (contents, date) in a cool, dark place; remove bands.

## Caution
Water-bath canning is only for high-acid foods. Vegetables, meat, fish, beans and soups are low-acid and must be pressure canned ([[G-19]]) or the botulism toxin can grow in the jar with no smell or bulge. Tomatoes need added acid: USDA calls for 2 tablespoons bottled lemon juice or ½ teaspoon citric acid per quart.

## Field note
Hudsonville is below 1,000 feet elevation, so USDA's base processing times (0–1,000 ft) apply — no altitude adjustment needed. Throw out any jar with a bulging lid, spurting liquid, mold, or off smell without tasting it.
