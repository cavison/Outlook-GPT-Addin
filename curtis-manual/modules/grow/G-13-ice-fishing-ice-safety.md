Ice fishing feeds families in a Michigan winter — but only on ice you have measured, never alone.

## You need
- Spud bar or auger, tape measure or marked stick
- Ice picks worn around the neck, rope (50 ft), life jacket or float coat
- Short jig rod or handline ([[G-09]]), small jigs, waxworms or minnows
- Bucket to sit on, skimmer or slotted spoon
- Warm layers, dry spare gloves and socks
- Michigan fishing license (age 17+)

## Steps
1. **Tell someone** where you are going and when you'll be back. Go with a buddy.
2. **Test as you go:** strike the ice with a spud bar every few steps; if it goes through, turn back the way you came.
3. **Measure:** drill a test hole near shore and measure. Commonly cited guidance is at least 4 inches of clear, solid ice for one person walking.
4. **Read the color:** clear blue/black ice is strongest. White, gray, slushy or honeycombed ice is weak — stay off.
5. **Avoid danger spots:** inlets, outlets, bridges, pressure cracks, docks, and areas with current or springs.
6. **Fish:** drill a hole, jig a small lure tipped with a waxworm near bottom, then lift slowly.
7. **If you fall in:** stay calm, turn toward the way you came, kick and pull yourself out with ice picks, roll away from the hole — do not stand up.

## Caution
Michigan DNR says no ice is ever 100% safe. Ice thickness varies across one lake, and river ice (like the Grand River) is weakened by current. Never go on ice that is gray, slushy, or has open water nearby.

## Field note
Snow on top insulates the ice and slows freezing, and it hides weak spots — new ice under fresh snow can be much thinner than ice nearby. Late-season ice can look thick but rot from the inside; "honeycombed" ice in March can fail suddenly.
